Unit 12 · Reflection

Critical reflection

What I did, why it changed my understanding, and how I will work differently.

What?

From exercises to a team project

I use the “What? So what? Now what?” questions suggested by Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001) to structure this reflection.

In Unit 3, I wrote a Python scraper that retrieved and parsed a web page, then recorded the source and retrieval details with the extracted result. In Unit 4, I profiled a survey dataset before cleaning it, kept the raw files unchanged and documented how I selected a smaller sample. The Unit 5 Ebola investigation required a different kind of judgement: the source was recognisable, but more than half of the profiled cells were blank, and some cumulative values fell over time. I recorded those issues instead of treating every blank as zero or every decrease as an error. These activities are documented in my learning journey.

For the Unit 6 team proposal, I worked on conceptual and logical database design, creating entity-relationship diagrams with diagrams.net and dbdiagram.io. I explained the normalisation decisions: the flat healthcare source mixed patient, encounter, clinician, diagnosis and capacity information, so the proposed model centred on Encounter and separated repeating subjects and many-to-many relationships. I also corrected sections of the shared document where the wording overstated what the data established. In Unit 7, a separate normalisation exercise showed me that a tidy schema can still encode the wrong meaning if assumptions about an ambiguous exam score or varying exam boards are left unstated.

The team’s Unit 11 outcome went beyond the proposal: it included a traceable raw layer, relational SQLite database, validation rules and interactive dashboard. The final report records 10,000 source rows, of which 4,750 were quarantined because checkout preceded check-in. The dashboard’s default population was therefore 5,250 encounters. Its 42.11% positive source readmission indicator could not be called a clinical readmission rate because the data supplied neither a readmission window nor a link to an earlier encounter. Comparing this outcome with my initial design showed me that implementing a proposed schema is only one part of producing trustworthy analysis.

So what?

What the problems taught me

The constant lesson was that the notion of data quality involves more than just formatting. It is entirely possible to write a script that finds no blank cells and to run integrity checks on the database, yet the data remains contradictory. In the team outcome, 76 relational checks, 25 feature checks and 22 dashboard-foundation checks passed. That was valuable evidence that calculations and relationships worked as designed, but it did not validate what “readmission”, waiting time or satisfaction meant in the source. I now distinguish a verified computation from a verified interpretation.

So many quarantined records caused me to have second thoughts regarding the extent to which I could depend on the dataset. Although passing tests provided some comfort, they did not solve the problems that originated from the source of the data. This dilemma made me more cautious in distinguishing the two.

The separate survey header dictionary was necessary to understand abbreviated fields, and I chose not to impute missing values without a rule grounded in their meaning. In Unit 5, I kept historical reporting changes visible rather than forcing a smooth series. The Unit 7 exercise then made me explain where a score belonged before choosing a table. Together these experiences changed how I approach extraction, exploration, cleaning and modelling: I need to establish provenance and definitions before automating transformations.

Discussion with classmates extended this learning beyond code. In my GDPR post and response, I considered why replacing an identifier does not automatically anonymise data and why a proportionate security measure depends on the risk and context. A peer response helped me connect those principles to access control and minimisation during data preparation itself. I also found that my initial account of the British Airways and Marriott enforcement cases described an outdated stage of the process so correcting that point reinforced the need to check the date and status of a cited claim, not merely whether a source looks credible. My discussion record separates the response I wrote from feedback I received.

Reviewing the project document gave me a concrete example of why wording matters. I challenged a draft sentence saying “41.42% were classified as readmissions” because the data supported only “41.42% of records contain a positive value in the supplied readmission indicator”. That correction changed the strength of the claim, even if it did not change the calculation. The later Unit 11 dashboard reports 42.11% for its filtered default population, with a different denominator; these figures should not be conflated. Explaining normalisation to the team was similar: I needed to show why tables and keys reflected the data’s relationships, not simply present a diagram. These contributions taught me that careful documentation and peer review are part of technical quality. The completed system still requires a data owner to define readmission events and investigate capacity anomalies before clinical use.

Now what?

Changes I will carry forward

In my future work, I will retain a layer of original sources, clarify meaning and assumptions behind field values, and perform data validation prior to choosing which records should be included in the analysis. I will note both why records were excluded and how they impact the denominator. In case where a measure is defined unclearly, I will retain the label from the source and avoid making any stronger clinical or operational claims about its meaning until there is a confirmation from someone in charge of the definition of the measure.

In collaborative work, I will make my responsibilities, design explanations, document corrections and handovers easier to trace. The portfolio records my diagrams and review contribution, while the final report remains a team product; meeting notes and links to relevant artefacts would make that division clearer. I will also invite feedback earlier, because a classmate’s discussion response exposed a practical implication I had not considered. My next priority is to combine technical validation with clearer communication of uncertainty, privacy risks and the conditions under which an analytical output is safe to use.
